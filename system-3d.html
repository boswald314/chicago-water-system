Scale

Horizontal distances are always true — nothing here stretches them. The system is about 50 km across and 90 m deep, so at 1:1 it reads as a sheet, and an 8-metre tunnel is a hair. The vertical slider stretches the whole vertical axis, the standard geologic-section convention: depths, tank heights and reservoir depths all grow together, so shapes are honest in plan but taller than life. The conduit slider widens pipe and shaft radii only, and never touches reservoir, tank or building dimensions. How fast the water moves

Nothing in the model moves at a uniform speed. Every animated flow is driven by a computed velocity:

  • Tunnels — flow divided by the wetted area of the bore at that exact point, so a nearly empty 33-foot tunnel runs fast and the same tunnel running full runs slow.
  • Drop shafts — a plunge, drawn at an energy-dissipated design value rather than free fall, which over 250 ft would reach about 130 ft/s. The "Chicago style" plunge shaft exists precisely to destroy that energy.
  • Force mains — flow over the riser's own area, moving upward while the pumps run.
  • Treatment plants — each unit process holds the water for its volume divided by the flow, so the particles crawl through the aeration basin for hours and dart along the channels between tanks. The residence time is printed on each stage and it shortens as the storm load rises, which is exactly what happens in the real plant.
  • CSO outfalls — the discharge each station is rated to pass, over the area that rating implies. Once more arrives than the station can pass, the velocity stops rising and the surplus is reported as backing up the collection system instead.

Recorded storms and the comparison

Event ranking: multi-day runs of wet days at O'Hare and Midway (NCEI GHCN-Daily), ranked by the larger gauge total, 2006–2026. Ten ASOS stations sit 15 to 40 km apart, which is too coarse for a convective storm, so the gauge field is built from four networks: the Iowa Environmental Mesonet's ASOS archive, NCEI's co-op hourly gauges, USGS tipping buckets, and the GHCN-Daily co-op and CoCoRaHS gauges, which contribute a measured event total and borrow their timing from the nearest hourly gauge. That is 11 to 130 gauges per storm instead of ten. Each basin is then rained by the area average of the inverse-distance field over a grid of points inside its own outline, not by the field at its centroid — so rain that falls on the separate-sewered suburbs beyond a basin's edge stays out of it. The comparison is against MWRD's own pumping-station discharge log, summed over the event window plus two days. The log covers only the six stations, not the gravity outfalls, so the model's "passed through the stations" figure is the like-for-like number. Across the ten storms the geometric-mean ratio of modelled to recorded is about 1.1, with individual storms from 0.6× to 2.8×.

Checking the model past the six pumping stations

The discharge log above covers six stations. It cannot say which gravity outfalls opened, whether the system was pushed to reverse into the lake, or where water stood. Three further records, assembled per storm in map-data/storm-validation.json, can — none of them covering all ten storms:

  • MWRD's CSO Event Synopsis Report (apps.mwrd.org/csoreports) — every outfall's tide-gate open and close timestamp, with its waterway reach and receiving plant, plus MWRD's own three-basin rain-gauge totals. The earliest selectable date is 1 April 2016, so of the ten storms only October 2017 and May 2020 are covered. Only days on which a CSO happened appear in its date list, which makes an empty window inside the covered period a recorded absence of overflow rather than a gap. For both covered storms MWRD logged overflows in 15–16 reaches across the North Shore Channel, North Branch, Des Plaines, Salt Creek and Ship Canal — and in no Calumet-system reach at all, which is what the model predicts for both.
  • MWRD's "Reversals to Lake Michigan (1985–Present)" — the dated, per-structure ledger of every occasion the lakefront gates or locks were opened and the river ran backwards into the lake, in million gallons at O'Brien, the Chicago River Controlling Works and Wilmette. Nine of the ten storms have a reversal inside their window; August 2014 has none, and the table shows that year's only event was in June, so this is a true negative rather than a missing row. Reversal is a last-resort act, not a measure of overflow, but it ranks the storms by how hard the system was pressed: the model's total CSO volume ranks them the same way to a Spearman correlation of about +0.88, against +0.59 for rainfall alone.
  • Chicago 311 service requests — "Water in Basement" and "Water On Street", the only direct observation of where water actually stood. The city's 311 dataset begins in July 2018 and these two request types first appear on 19 December 2018, so of the ten storms only May 2020 is covered; the twelve historical 311 extracts the city publishes for 2011–2018 are all non-water categories. In that one storm, basement complaints run from about ten a day to 713 on 17 May, and cluster on the far Northwest Side — the same Des Plaines and upper North Branch outfalls that dominate MWRD's own gate log for those days.

Nulls in that file mean not found, never zero, and the coverage flags differ per source and per storm. No per-storm TARP capture volume, reservoir or tunnel fill, or flooded-basement count was found for any of the ten: MWRD publishes those only as multi-year cumulative totals, which are carried in the file's meta.context with their quotes.

Where the standing-water layer fails. Only one of the ten storms falls inside 311 coverage, so the check was repeated on eight further rain events ranked from the same gauges by the same rule, all of them after December 2018. The model predicts zero standing water for seven of the eight — including the two worst basement floods in the entire 311 record, 17 August 2025 (3,783 water-in-basement complaints in one day) and 2 July 2023 (the storm MWRD says flooded more than 70,000 homes and forced 1,142.6 million gallons back into the lake). In both, the model's tunnels fill but McCook never spills, so nothing overflows and nothing pools. The rank correlation against 311 basement complaints is therefore about +0.25, which is no better than rainfall alone. Two causes, and they compound: the gauges are 10–30 miles apart and genuinely miss a convective cell sitting over the West Side, and the model has no conveyance limit upstream of the interceptors — it assumes every drop reaches TARP, when in reality the neighbourhood sewers surcharge and back into basements long before the reservoirs are full. The standing-water layer should be read as "where the surplus would go if the collection system could deliver it," not as a flood forecast. Against the harder, coarser observable — how much water MWRD was forced to send back into Lake Michigan — the model does far better, ranking the ten storms at about +0.88.

geometric-mean ratio of modelled to recorded is about 1.25, with individual storms from 0.6× to 2.6×. That ratio sat nearer 1.0 while the model was reading ten ASOS gauges: the denser field finds more rain than the airports did, so the 0.32 runoff coefficient — fitted back when the rain was under-sampled — is now carrying a bias that belongs to the runoff, not the rainfall, and is due a fresh calibration.

Standing water

When a basin's overflow exceeds what its outfalls are rated to pass, the surplus surcharges the collection system and stands in the streets. The model pools that volume in the basin's lowest ground from the same terrain grid as the contours (a stage–storage curve from the real DEM), and drains it back out through the outfalls once they have room. It is where the water goes, not a hydraulic flood map: no street network, no curbs, no time for it to get there.

Ground contours

10-foot elevation contours from AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen terrarium encoding, USGS NED/3DEP underneath), sampled at about 160 m, smoothed and simplified. Lake Michigan sits at ~579 ft, so the 580-ft line is effectively the shoreline; the far-west moraine climbs past 750 ft. The model still draws the ground flat — Chicago's relief is small next to the 150–300 ft tunnel depth — so these are reference lines only, labelled where you zoom in.

Real velocities here span four orders of magnitude, from about 0.01 ft/s creeping through a final settling tank to a plunge of 26 ft/s down a shaft. Animating them literally would leave the basins frozen and the shafts teleporting, so the on-screen speed is proportional to the square root of the computed velocity. The ordering and the relative differences survive; the computed value in ft/s is printed on the element, so the real number is never hidden behind the animation.

The simulation

A mass balance, not a hydraulic model. It conserves volume across the published storage capacities, plant design flows and pump capacities, and it routes each basin's excess down the tunnel system that actually serves it. It does not solve for head, velocity, air entrainment, surge or travel time, so it will not tell you when a particular shaft chokes or where a pressure wave goes. Its one fitted parameter is the runoff coefficient, set to 0.32 because that is the value that reproduces MWRD's recorded pumping-station discharge for 13 September 2008.
